Key Terms

Urbanization
City building and the movement of people to cities (p. 723).
Middle class
A social class of skilled workers, professionals, businesspeople, and wealthy farmers (p. 725).
Child labor
Children working jobs, often long hours in dangerous places, for very low pay.
Social mobility
The ability to move up (or down) from one social class to another.

Cotton: From the Fields to the Factories

Manchester's mills ran on cotton. But cotton won't grow in Britain's cool, wet weather. So where did it come from?

By the early 1800s, most of Britain's cotton came from plantations in the American South, where enslaved people planted and picked it. In 1793, Eli Whitney invented the cotton gin, a machine that cleaned seeds out of cotton much faster. Now planters could sell all the cotton they could grow, so they wanted more land and more enslaved workers. American cotton production jumped from 1.5 million pounds in 1790 to 85 million pounds in 1810. By 1830, roughly 2 million people were enslaved in the United States.

Life on a cotton plantation was brutal. Enslaved people worked from sunrise to sunset, often under the threat of the whip. Families could be sold apart at any time. Enslaved people were bought and sold as property, as if they weren't human. Treating people as less than human is called dehumanization.

Here's the connection. Bales of cotton were shipped across the Atlantic to Liverpool. Then they traveled by canal, and later by railroad, to the mills of Manchester. Workers there, including children, spun and wove the cotton into cloth. Britain sold that cloth all over the world. Cheap cotton clothing made life better for millions of people. But it was built on the exploitation of enslaved people an ocean away.

Britain banned the slave trade in 1807 and ended slavery in most of its colonies in 1833. Even so, British mills kept buying cotton grown by enslaved people in the United States until the American Civil War in the 1860s. Other raw materials for British industry, like sugar and some metals, also came from enslaved or forced workers.

The African Diaspora

The slave trade scattered millions of Africans and their descendants across the Americas, Europe, and beyond. This forced scattering is called the African Diaspora. In Africa, many communities lost generations of young people, and countless families were torn apart forever. Their histories were often erased.

In the Americas, enslaved Africans and their descendants built much of the wealth of places like Haiti, Barbados, and the United States. They also brought farming knowledge, music, art, religion, and food that still shape life in the Americas today. Many countries, from the United States to Brazil, have large populations of African descent.
